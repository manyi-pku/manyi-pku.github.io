Active Janus Particles in Viscoelastic Fluids

Viscoelastic biofluids — the natural habitat of many swimmers

Self-diffusiophoretic propulsion — an asymmetric coating sets up a self-generated solute gradient that drives motion

How does fluid elasticity change the propulsion speed itself?

Many biological fluids are viscoelastic such as mucus, blood, and biofilms. And these are exactly the fluids where many microswimmers live. Since swimming relies on the interaction with the surrounding fluid, the elasticity of that fluid can directly change how a swimmer moves.

In today’s discussion, I focus on a model swimmer, the self-diffusiophoretic Janus particle. One half is catalytically active, and the other half is inert. The active half drives a chemical reaction that sets up a solute gradient around the particle, and this gradient drives it forward. So the particle propels itself, with no moving parts.

This kind of propulsion is already well understood in a Newtonian fluid. So the question is simple. How does fluid elasticity change the propulsion speed itself?

Gomez-Solano et al., PRL, 2016

Propulsion reduced in polymer solutions

Saad & Natale, Soft Matter, 2019

Active motion suppressed in polymer solutions

Raman et al., ACS Phys. Chem. Au, 2023

Experiments reveal rich but noise-dominated dynamics — the intrinsic propulsion speed is hard to isolate. To probe it cleanly, we turn to theory.

Over the past decade, experiments have shown that viscoelastic fluids reshape these swimmers in several ways.

First, faster motion was found to give stronger rotational diffusion. Second, a later study found that propulsion is reduced in polymer solutions. And third, more recent work found that the active motion is suppressed, even in dilute polymer solutions.

These experiments tell us something important. Viscoelasticity has a strong effect on these swimmers. But the measured speed mixes many effects together, such as polymer caging, entanglement, and orientation fluctuations. So it is hard to separate out the intrinsic role of viscoelasticity, that is, whether elasticity itself makes the particle faster or slower. This intrinsic effect is exactly what we would like to understand.

For a half-coated sphere, the leading-order speed correction vanishes.

But what if the Janus particle is not spherical?

To have a clean answer, we turn to theory. There are many constitutive models, such as Oldroyd-B or Giesekus. But when elasticity is weak, all of them reduce to the same form: the second-order fluid. In theory, this model is often used to capture viscoelastic effects as the first correction to a Newtonian fluid.

Earlier work studied exactly this case, a spherical Janus particle, and found two things. First, the first-order viscoelastic correction is antisymmetric in the surface coverage. Second, and as a direct result, for a half-coated sphere this leading-order correction vanishes.

So this gives us a clean baseline and leads to a question, what if the Janus particle is not spherical?

From Spheres to Spheroids

Sphere — correction vanishes at half-coating

?

Spheroid — geometric anisotropy

We vary two control parameters: eccentricity \(e\) (shape) and coverage \(\zeta_0\) (activity).

To answer this, we let the particle become a spheroid. This is the natural way to add geometric anisotropy while keeping the problem analytically tractable.

Model: a spheroidal self-diffusiophoretic particle

  • Solve the concentration and flow fields in prolate spheroidal coordinates \((\tau,\zeta)\)
  • Shape parameter: eccentricity \(e=\sqrt{a^2-b^2}/a\)
  • Activity parameter: catalytic coverage \(\zeta_0\)

We consider a rigid prolate spheroid, and we solve both the concentration field and the flow field in prolate spheroidal coordinates, tau and zeta. Then the particle surface is conveniently a surface of constant tau.

Two parameters define the problem. The shape is set by the eccentricity e. When e is zero we have a sphere, and as e approaches one the particle becomes a slender rod. The activity is set by the catalytic coverage, which marks the boundary between the active and inert regions.

Concentration gradients drive surface slip

Surface flux \(\to\) Concentration field

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla C = D\nabla^2 C, \quad \left. D\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\nabla C \right|_{\tau=\tau_0,\zeta<\zeta_0} = -A \]

\(C\): solute concentration; \(\boldsymbol{u}\): fluid velocity; \(D\): solute diffusivity; \(A\): surface activity / fixed solute flux on the active cap; \(\boldsymbol{n}\): unit normal.

Tangential gradient \(\to\) Phoretic slip

\[ \boldsymbol{u}_s = M(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{n})\cdot \nabla C \]

\(M\): phoretic mobility; \((\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{n})\): tangential projection operator.

Slip velocity \(\to\) Force-free propulsion

\[ \boldsymbol{u}(\tau=\tau_0) = \boldsymbol{u}_s(\zeta)+\boldsymbol{U}, \]

\[ \int_S \boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}S = \boldsymbol{0} \]

biological propulsion analogy

Propulsion arises in three steps.

First, the chemistry sets up a concentration field. The solute concentration follows an advection-diffusion equation. The boundary condition is on the particle surface: the active cap emits solute at a fixed flux, which we call A, while the inert part is impermeable. Solving this gives a non-uniform solute field around the particle.

Second, this concentration gradient drives a slip velocity in a thin interaction layer at the surface. We assume the slip is tangential, and proportional to the local tangential gradient, with the coefficient being the phoretic mobility, M. This slip comes from a boundary-layer analysis of that thin layer, so it acts as an effective boundary condition for the outer flow.

Third, the surface slip sets the particle in motion. On the surface, the fluid velocity is the slip plus the rigid-body swimming velocity, U. And because the particle is force-free, the total hydrodynamic force on it is zero. This condition is what determines the swimming speed U. This is the same mechanism by which ciliated microorganisms swim.

Fluid rheology: second-order fluid model

Flow equations

\[ \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{0}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0, \qquad \boldsymbol{\sigma}=-p\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{T} \]

\(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\): total stress; \(p\): pressure; \(\boldsymbol{T}\): deviatoric stress.

Second-order fluid model

\[ \boldsymbol{T} = \mu\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}} -\frac{\Psi_1}{2}\boldsymbol{G} +\Psi_2\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}, \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{G} =& \boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}} -(\nabla\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}} -\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u} \end{aligned} \]

\(\mu\): viscosity; \(\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\): shear-rate tensor; \(\Psi_1,\Psi_2\): first and second normal-stress coefficients; \(\boldsymbol{G}\): steady upper-convected derivative.

Non-dimensionalization

length scale: semi-major axis of the particle \(a\),

velocity scale: \(MA/D\)

Peclet number (advection/diffusion): \(\mathrm{Pe}=\dfrac{MAa}{D^2}\)

Deborah number (fluid elasticity): \(\mathrm{De}=\dfrac{MA\Psi_1}{2\mu D a}\)

Now the fluid model. As I mentioned, we use the second-order fluid. The flow is incompressible Stokes flow, and the stress has a Newtonian part plus the elastic correction. The elastic part is set by the two normal-stress coefficients.

We then non-dimensionalize. We use the particle size as the length scale, and the characteristic slip speed, which is set by the flux A and the mobility M, as the velocity scale. This brings out two dimensionless numbers. The Peclet number, Pe, which compares advection and diffusion of the solute. And the Deborah number, De, which measures the strength of elasticity.

Diffusion-dominated solute transport

Diffusion-dominated solute field

\[ \nabla^2 c=0, \qquad \left.\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\nabla c\right|_{\tau=\tau_0} = \begin{cases} -1, & \zeta\le \zeta_0 \quad \text{active},\\ 0, & \zeta>\zeta_0 \quad \text{inert}, \end{cases} \qquad c|_{\tau\to\infty}=0 \]

\(c\): dimensionless relative solute concentration; \(\tau=\tau_0\): particle surface.

Series solution and induced slip

\[ c(\tau,\zeta) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \rho_n Q_n(\tau)P_n(\zeta) \]

\[ \implies u_s(\zeta) = \tau_0\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n\frac{P_n^1(\zeta)}{\sqrt{\tau_0^2-\zeta^2}} \]

\(P_n,Q_n\): Legendre functions; \(\rho_n\): coefficients set by the flux boundary condition; \(B_n=-\rho_n Q_n(\tau_0)\).

Note: finite-\(\mathrm{Pe}\) coupling

At finite \(\mathrm{Pe}\), advection couples the solute field back to the flow. Then the rheology can also affect the concentration and the slip generated inside the interaction layer.

Choudhary et al., J. Fluid Mech., 2020

We further take the diffusion-dominated limit, that is, small Peclet number. This is a big simplification: the solute decouples from the flow. The concentration field then satisfies Laplace’s equation, with a flux boundary condition that is constant on the active cap and zero on the inert part. We solve it as a series of Legendre functions, and from it we get the surface slip directly.

In this limit, the slip is set entirely by the concentration field, so it is the same as in a Newtonian fluid. We note that at finite Pe this is no longer true: advection couples the solute back to the flow, and the rheology can also change the slip. Obviously, that is a more complicated problem, which we set aside here so that we can isolate the hydrodynamic effect of elasticity.

Newtonian baseline

Weakly viscoelastic: expand in \(\mathrm{De}\ll 1\)

\[ \{\boldsymbol{u}, p, \boldsymbol{U}\} = \{\boldsymbol{u}_0, p_0, \boldsymbol{U}_0\} + \mathrm{De}\{\boldsymbol{u}_1, p_1, \boldsymbol{U}_1\} + \mathcal{O}(\mathrm{De}^2) \]

The zeroth order is the Newtonian problem, valid for any eccentricity, with a known speed:

\[ U_0 = -\frac{\tau_0}{2} \int_{-1}^{1} u_s \frac{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}{\sqrt{\tau_0^2-\zeta^2}} \,\mathrm{d}\zeta \]

Poehnl et al., J. Phys.: Condens. Matter, 2020; Popescu et al., Eur. Phys. J. E, 2010

Linearity makes the Newtonian speed symmetric about half-coating: \(U_0(\zeta_0)=U_0(-\zeta_0)\).

Now we set up the perturbation. We consider small De, and we expand the quantities in orders of De.

The zeroth order is just the Newtonian problem, which has been solved before, so we take it as known. One feature of the Newtonian solution is that the speed is symmetric about the coating parameter. We can understand this using a simple symmetry. The fully-coated problem does not swim, and it can be separated into a pair of complementary coated problems. Because the Newtonian problem is linear, the speeds of the particle pair must be the same.

First-order viscoelastic correction

Viscoelasticity enters as a forcing term, set by the Newtonian flow:

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}_1 = -p_1\boldsymbol{I} + \dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_1 + \boldsymbol{A}, \qquad \boldsymbol{A} = -(\boldsymbol{G}_0 + b\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0) \]

An auxiliary towing problem + the reciprocal theorem give \(U_1\) directly, without solving the first-order flow:

\[ U_1 = -\frac{(\tau_0^2+1)\operatorname{coth}^{-1}\tau_0-\tau_0}{4\tau_0^2} \int_{\tau_0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1} (\boldsymbol{A}:\nabla\hat{\boldsymbol{u}})(\tau^2-\zeta^2)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\tau \]

\(\nabla\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\): velocity gradient of the auxiliary (towing) flow.

Now the first-order correction. Viscoelasticity enters as a forcing term, and this forcing is built entirely from the known Newtonian flow. So there is nothing new to solve there.

To get the speed correction, we use the standard method. We introduce an auxiliary towing problem and apply the reciprocal theorem, and then the correction is written as an integral. Compared with earlier work, nothing is new here. The only difference is that the calculation is carried out in the spheroidal coordinates. With this one integral, we can now study how shape and coverage control the viscoelastic correction.

The spherical cancellation is a symmetry

In a second-order fluid, a mirror reflection must be paired with De → −De:

\[U(\zeta_0, \mathrm{De}) = U(-\zeta_0, -\mathrm{De})\]

Half-coating is its own mirror ⟹ U even in De: the O(De) correction vanishes, leading correction is O(De²).

We first revisit the spherical case. The known conclusion is that the viscoelastic correction is antisymmetric in the surface activity. Here we can explain this from a hidden symmetry. Although the flow field is no longer linear, the slip velocity is solved from the concentration, so the slip fields can still be added up. For a sphere, a fully-coated particle has zero slip everywhere, due to the isotropy. So the boundary velocities of a particle pair have a symmetry.

We then ask: do they swim at the same speed? In a Newtonian fluid, yes. But the second-order fluid is more subtle. Its elastic stress carries a directional memory, so a mirror reflection leaves the problem unchanged only if we also flip the sign of De. The two speeds therefore match with De reversed. Consequently, the half-coated case is its own mirror image, so the speed must be even in De. This means the leading correction is at least of order De squared. Through this argument, we show that the symmetry really requires the isotropy of the sphere. Once the shape is not spherical, we expect that this symmetry no longer holds.

Eccentricity revives the correction

Half-coated particle: \(\zeta_0=0\)

  • Sphere: \(U_1=0\), protected by symmetry
  • Spheroid: eccentricity breaks the cancellation
  • Slender particles: pronounced speed enhancement

Here are the results. We consider half-coated particles with different shapes, and plot the speed scaled by the Newtonian speed. For the sphere, the correction is zero. But as we increase the eccentricity, it first slows down a little bit, and then for very slender spheroids there is a pronounced enhancement. So our expectation is confirmed.

Breaking the coverage symmetry

Antisymmetry lost as \(e\) grows

Phase diagram: enhancement vs reduction

Eccentricity breaks the coverage antisymmetry, and the enhancement regime broadens with elongation.

Next we also consider the coverage. The spherical case is represented by the black curve, the deviation is antisymmetric about half-coating, as we discussed. But as the eccentricity grows, this antisymmetry is lost. Shape breaks the coverage symmetry.

The right panel is the full phase diagram, over eccentricity and coverage. The color shows whether the speed is enhanced or reduced. As the particle becomes more elongated, the enhancement region broadens. Especially when the particle is very slender, in most cases the propulsion is enhanced.

Why the cancellation breaks

  • The first-order speed is set by a local kernel: \(U_1 \propto \int_V\) 𝒦 \(\mathrm{d}V\), where 𝒦 \(= \boldsymbol{A}:\nabla\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\) (blue enhances, red reduces)

Sphere — 𝒦 antisymmetric, cancels exactly ⟹ \(U_1=0\)

Spheroid — curvature localizes 𝒦, antisymmetry broken ⟹ \(U_1\neq0\)

Finally, we would like to visualize what happens around the particle. Since the first-order speed correction is a volume integral, we just plot the integral kernel, K. Blue regions push the speed up, and red regions push it down. For the sphere, on the left, the Newtonian flow is symmetric about the equator, so all the blue and red regions cancel out exactly. However, for a spheroidal particle, we no longer have this symmetry. Particularly near the highly curved tip, the slip changes quickly, and the flow gradients become large. This localizes the kernel and breaks its antisymmetry. So the cancellation is no longer exact, and a net enhancement survives. And these are all the main results.

Summary

  • Spheres — the half-coated correction vanishes, protected by reflection symmetry
  • Spheroids — eccentricity breaks it; slender particles are strongly enhanced
  • General principle — symmetry breaks only when both shape anisotropy and fluid nonlinearity are present

To summarize. For a sphere, we clarify that the vanishing correction is a special case, a result of the geometric isotropy of the sphere. Once the particle becomes a spheroid, eccentricity breaks this symmetry, and especially for slender particles, the propulsion is enhanced in most cases.

More generally, we believe in a principle: symmetry breaking requires the combination of geometric anisotropy and fluid nonlinearity. In short, particle shape is a control parameter for propulsion in complex fluids. Thank you, and I am happy to take questions.
