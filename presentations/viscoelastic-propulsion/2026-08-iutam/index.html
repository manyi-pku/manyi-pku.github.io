How does fluid rheology alter active propulsion?

Here, by propulsion, I mean the motion of self-propelled active particles. These can be biological swimmers, such as microorganisms, or synthetic systems, such as microrobots and Janus particles. I will introduce what a Janus particle is in a moment.

Many of them move in complex fluids. For example, H. pylori swims through the mucus layer in the stomach, and sperm cells move through cervical mucus. So the surrounding fluid is often very different from a simple Newtonian liquid. Since the propulsion comes from interacting with the surrounding fluid, its rheology can strongly affect how these particles move.

So the question is simple: how does fluid rheology alter active propulsion?

What Is a Janus Particle?

Two distinct surface regions can enable self-propelled motion.

Video: “Janus particles,” Sikapaa TV, YouTube

In this talk, we focus on Janus particles, a widely studied class of synthetic self-propelled particles. They are used as simple model systems for active matter. They are also explored for microscale transport and microrobotics.

Here we can roughly see that the particle has two distinct surface regions. Let me briefly explain how a Janus particle propels itself.

How Does a Janus Particle Propel Itself?

Chemical reaction

creates an uneven solute concentration

Surface concentration gradient

generates a thin surface flow (slip)

Asymmetric slip

produces net propulsion

Self-diffusiophoresis: asymmetric activity → solute gradient → surface slip → propulsion

A classic Janus particle is a sphere with two different surface regions. Typically, one side is coated with a catalyst, while the other side is inert.

When the particle is placed in a solution, the active side drives a chemical reaction. This creates an uneven solute concentration around the particle.

As the solute interacts with the particle surface, a concentration gradient along the surface generates a tangential flow near the surface, which we normally describe as surface slip. This asymmetric surface slip finally gives rise to a net propulsion of the particle. This type of propulsion is usually called self-diffusiophoresis.

Now we know the basic propulsion mechanism. In a simple Newtonian fluid, this picture is relatively well understood. But in a complex fluid, the surrounding medium can respond very differently to the same surface motion.

Experiments reveal diverse responses, but the direct rheological effect on propulsion remains unclear.

Over the past decade, experiments have shown that complex fluids can change Janus-particle dynamics in several ways.

Some studies found enhanced rotational fluctuations. Others reported reduced propulsion. More recent work showed that active motion can be strongly suppressed, even in dilute polymer solutions.

These experiments clearly show that the surrounding fluid matters. But they measure the overall particle dynamics, where propulsion and rotational motion are coupled together. So it is difficult to isolate the direct effect of rheology on propulsion itself. This is what we would like to understand theoretically.

The first is shear thinning. As the shear rate increases, the apparent viscosity decreases. Blood is a familiar example, as shown here.

The second is viscoelasticity. I actually couldn’t find a really intuitive quantitative figure for this — if anyone knows a good one, please let me know. Spherical Particles: What Does Theory Predict?

So what does theory already tell us about these two rheological effects? Previous work considered spherical Janus particles.

In a shear-thinning fluid, the correction is negative, so shear thinning slows the particle. In a weakly viscoelastic fluid, the first-order correction is antisymmetric in the surface coverage. As a direct result, this correction vanishes for a half-coated sphere.

So these results give us a clean baseline and lead to one question. Are they general consequences of rheology, or are they special to spherical geometry?

Spheroid — geometric anisotropy

We vary two control parameters: eccentricity \(e\) (shape) and coverage \(\zeta_0\) (activity).

To answer this, we let the particle become a spheroid. This is the natural way to add geometric anisotropy while keeping the problem analytically tractable.

We then vary two control parameters. The eccentricity sets the shape, and the catalytic coverage sets the activity.

Model: A Spheroidal Self-Diffusiophoretic Particle

  • Solve the concentration and flow fields in prolate spheroidal coordinates \((\tau,\zeta)\)
  • Shape parameter: eccentricity \(e=\sqrt{a^2-b^2}/a\)
  • Activity parameter: catalytic coverage \(\zeta_0\)

We consider a rigid prolate spheroid, and we solve both the concentration field and the flow field in prolate spheroidal coordinates, tau and zeta. Then the particle surface is conveniently a surface of constant tau.

Two parameters define the problem. The shape is set by the eccentricity e. When e is zero we have a sphere, and as e approaches one the particle becomes a slender rod. The activity is set by the catalytic coverage, which marks the boundary between the active and inert regions.

Concentration Gradients Drive Surface Slip

Surface flux \(\to\) Concentration field

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla C = D\nabla^2 C, \quad \left. D\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\nabla C \right|_{\tau=\tau_0,\zeta<\zeta_0} = -A \]

\(C\): solute concentration; \(\boldsymbol{u}\): fluid velocity; \(D\): solute diffusivity; \(A\): surface activity / fixed solute flux on the active cap; \(\boldsymbol{n}\): unit normal.

Tangential gradient \(\to\) Phoretic slip

\[ \boldsymbol{u}_s = M(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{n})\cdot \nabla C \]

\(M\): phoretic mobility; \((\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{n})\): tangential projection operator.

Slip velocity \(\to\) Force-free propulsion

\[ \boldsymbol{u}(\tau=\tau_0) = \boldsymbol{u}_s(\zeta)+\boldsymbol{U}, \]

\[ \int_S \boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}S = \boldsymbol{0} \]

biological propulsion analogy

Propulsion arises in three steps.

First, the chemistry sets up a concentration field. The solute concentration follows an advection-diffusion equation. The boundary condition is on the particle surface. The active cap emits solute at a fixed flux, which we call A, while the inert part is impermeable. Solving this gives a non-uniform solute field around the particle.

Second, this concentration gradient drives a slip velocity in a thin interaction layer at the surface. We assume the slip is tangential and proportional to the local tangential gradient. The coefficient is the phoretic mobility, M. This slip comes from a boundary-layer analysis of that thin layer, so it acts as an effective boundary condition for the outer flow.

Third, the surface slip sets the particle in motion. On the surface, the fluid velocity is the slip plus the rigid-body swimming velocity, U. Because the particle is force-free, the total hydrodynamic force on it is zero. This condition determines the swimming speed U. This is the same mechanism by which ciliated microorganisms swim.

Fluid Rheology: Two Non-Newtonian Models

Stokes flow:

\[ \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}=0, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0, \qquad \boldsymbol{\sigma}=-p\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{T} \]

\[ \dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}} = \nabla\mathbf{u}+(\nabla\mathbf{u})^T \]

Shear thinning: Carreau model

\[ \mathbf{T} = \left[ \beta+(1-\beta) \left( 1+\frac{Cu^2}{2} \dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}:\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}} \right)^{(n-1)/2} \right] \dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}. \]

\(Cu\) — flow shear-rate / characteristic shear-rate ratio

\(\beta\) — high-/low-shear viscosity ratio

\(n\) — shear-thinning index

Viscoelasticity: Second-order fluid

\[ \mathbf{T} = \dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}} -De\left( \mathbf{G} +b\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}} \right). \]

\(De\) — elastic / viscous stress ratio

\(b\) — normal-stress ratio

\(\mathbf{G}\) — upper-convected derivative

We work in the Stokes-flow regime, so the rheology enters through the stress.

For shear thinning, we use the Carreau model. The viscosity depends on the local shear rate. Cu compares the characteristic shear rate of the flow with the shear rate where shear thinning becomes important. Beta is the high- to low-shear viscosity ratio, and n controls how strongly the viscosity changes.

For viscoelasticity, we use a second-order fluid for weak viscoelasticity. The stress is the Newtonian stress plus a small elastic correction. The main parameter is the Deborah number, De, which compares the relaxation time with the flow time.

The correction contains an upper-convected derivative and a normal-stress contribution. As Professor Stone explained on Sunday, the convected derivative accounts for material change relative to the deformation imposed by the flow.

Diffusion-Dominated Solute Transport

Diffusion-dominated limit:   \(Pe=\dfrac{MAa}{D^2}\) — advection / diffusion

\[ Pe\ll1 \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla^2 c=0, \qquad \left.\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\nabla c\right|_{\tau=\tau_0} = \begin{cases} -1, & \zeta\le \zeta_0 \quad \text{active},\\ 0, & \zeta>\zeta_0 \quad \text{inert}, \end{cases} \qquad c|_{\tau\to\infty}=0 \]

\(c\): dimensionless relative solute concentration; \(\tau=\tau_0\): particle surface.

Series solution and induced slip

\[ c(\tau,\zeta) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \rho_n Q_n(\tau)P_n(\zeta) \]

\[ \implies u_s(\zeta) = \tau_0\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n\frac{P_n^1(\zeta)}{\sqrt{\tau_0^2-\zeta^2}} \]

\(P_n,Q_n\): Legendre functions; \(\rho_n\): coefficients set by the flux boundary condition; \(B_n=-\rho_n Q_n(\tau_0)\).

Note: finite-\(\mathrm{Pe}\) coupling

Finite \(Pe\): advection couples the solute and flow fields, allowing rheology to modify the concentration field and hence the phoretic slip.

Choudhary et al., J. Fluid Mech., 2020

We further take the diffusion-dominated limit, that is, small Peclet number. This is a big simplification because the solute decouples from the flow. The concentration field then satisfies Laplace’s equation, with a flux boundary condition that is constant on the active cap and zero on the inert part. We solve it as a series of Legendre functions, and from it we get the surface slip directly.

To be precise,

Newtonian Baseline

Weakly non-Newtonian: expand in \(\epsilon\ll 1\)

Shear thinning (Carreau): \(\epsilon = 1-\beta\)

Viscoelasticity (second-order fluid): \(\epsilon = \mathrm{De}\)

\[ \{\mathbf u,p,U\} = \{\mathbf u_0,p_0,U_0\} + \epsilon\{\mathbf u_1,p_1,U_1\} + O(\epsilon^2) \]

The zeroth order is the Newtonian problem, valid for any eccentricity, with a known speed:

\[ U_0 = -\frac{\tau_0}{2} \int_{-1}^{1} u_s \frac{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}{\sqrt{\tau_0^2-\zeta^2}} \,\mathrm{d}\zeta \]

Poehnl et al., J. Phys.: Condens. Matter, 2020; Popescu et al., Eur. Phys. J. E, 2010

Linearity makes the Newtonian speed symmetric about half-coating: \(U_0(\zeta_0)=U_0(-\zeta_0)\).

Now we set up the perturbation. We consider weakly non-Newtonian fluids, and we expand the quantities in orders of a small parameter, epsilon. For shear thinning, epsilon is one minus beta. For viscoelasticity, epsilon is De.

The zeroth order is just the Newtonian problem, which has been solved before, so we take it as known. One feature of the Newtonian solution is that the speed is symmetric about the coating parameter. We can understand this using a simple symmetry. The fully-coated problem does not swim, and it can be separated into a pair of complementary coated problems. Because the Newtonian problem is linear, the speeds of the particle pair must be the same.

First-Order Non-Newtonian Correction

Non-Newtonian rheology enters as a forcing set by the Newtonian flow:

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}_1 = -p_1\mathbf I + \dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_1 + \mathbf A \]

Shear thinning (Carreau)

\[ \color{#1f5f8f}{\mathbf A_{\mathrm{ST}}} = \left[ -1+ \left( 1+\frac{Cu^2}{2} \dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0:\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0 \right)^{(n-1)/2} \right] \dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0 \]

Viscoelasticity (second-order)

\[ \color{#a95724}{\mathbf A_{\mathrm{VE}}} = -\left( \mathbf G_0 + b\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0\cdot \dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0 \right) \]

An auxiliary towing problem + the reciprocal theorem give \(U_1\) directly, without solving the first-order flow:

\[ U_1 = -\frac{(\tau_0^2+1)\operatorname{coth}^{-1}\tau_0-\tau_0}{4\tau_0^2} \int_{\tau_0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1} (\boldsymbol{A}:\nabla\hat{\boldsymbol{u}})(\tau^2-\zeta^2)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\tau \]

\(\nabla\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\): velocity gradient of the auxiliary (towing) flow.

We now want to calculate the first-order correction to the propulsion.

In principle, we could solve the full first-order flow field, but that becomes a nonlinear problem and is quite cumbersome.

Instead, we use the reciprocal-theorem framework. It allows us to obtain the propulsion correction directly from the Newtonian solution, without solving the first-order flow.

Geometry Can Reverse the Effect of Shear Thinning

Half-coated particles: \(\zeta_0=0\)

  • Sphere: propulsion is always reduced
  • Slender spheroids: propulsion can be enhanced
  • Shape can reverse the rheological effect

G. Zhu, B. vanGogh, L. Zhu, O.S. Pak and Y. Man. JFM., 2024.

Here are the first results. We consider half-coated particles with different shapes, and plot the speed scaled by the Newtonian speed.

For the sphere, the speed is always reduced, as found in earlier work. But as the eccentricity increases, the trend changes. For a very slender spheroid, the speed rises above the Newtonian value over a range of Cu.

So geometric anisotropy can reverse the effect of shear thinning. Instead of slowing the particle, shear thinning can enhance its propulsion.

Geometry Breaks the Coverage Symmetry

Sphere

Complementary coatings → same speed

Spheroid

Complementary coatings → different speeds

Geometry breaks the complementary-coating symmetry.

Next, we compare different catalytic coverages.

For a sphere, two complementary coatings give the same propulsion speed. The curve is symmetric about half-coating. For a spheroid in a shear-thinning fluid, this symmetry is lost. Two complementary coatings can now give different speeds.

So shape changes not only the sign of the correction. It also changes the relation between activity patterns.

Why the Coverage Symmetry Breaks

Symmetry breaking requires both geometric anisotropy and nonlinear rheology.

We can understand this using the argument shown here.

For a fully-coated sphere, the surface slip is zero everywhere because of isotropy. Therefore, the two complementary coating problems have a reflection symmetry, even in a nonlinear fluid.

For a fully-coated spheroid, the local surface slip is not zero, although the net speed is still zero. The reflection symmetry is lost. In a Newtonian fluid, linearity still makes the speeds of the complementary pair equal. But in a shear-thinning fluid, we can no longer use this superposition.

So breaking the coverage symmetry requires both geometric anisotropy and nonlinear rheology. We will now see a related idea in a viscoelastic fluid.

The Spherical Cancellation Is a Symmetry

In a second-order fluid, a mirror reflection must be paired with De → −De:

\[U(\zeta_0, \mathrm{De}) = U(-\zeta_0, -\mathrm{De})\]

Half-coating is its own mirror ⟹ U even in De: the O(De) correction vanishes, leading correction is O(De²).

We first revisit the spherical case. The known conclusion is that the viscoelastic correction is antisymmetric in the surface activity. Here we can explain this from a hidden symmetry. Although the flow field is no longer linear, the slip velocity is solved from the concentration, so the slip fields can still be added up. For a sphere, a fully-coated particle has zero slip everywhere, due to the isotropy. So the boundary velocities of a particle pair have a symmetry.

We then ask: do they swim at the same speed? In a Newtonian fluid, yes. But the second-order fluid is more subtle. Its elastic stress carries a directional memory, so a mirror reflection leaves the problem unchanged only if we also flip the sign of De. The two speeds therefore match with De reversed. Consequently, the half-coated case is its own mirror image, so the speed must be even in De. This means the leading correction is at least of order De squared. Through this argument, we show that the symmetry really requires the isotropy of the sphere. Once the shape is not spherical, we expect that this symmetry no longer holds.

Eccentricity Revives the Correction

Half-coated particles: \(\zeta_0=0\)

  • Sphere → zero correction by symmetry
  • Increasing eccentricity → symmetry broken
  • Slender limit → strong propulsion enhancement

Q. Zhao, G. Zhu and Y. Man. (submitted).

Here are the results. We consider half-coated particles with different shapes, and plot the speed scaled by the Newtonian speed. For the sphere, the correction is zero. But as we increase the eccentricity, it first slows down a little bit, and then for very slender spheroids there is a pronounced enhancement. So our expectation is confirmed.

Why the Cancellation Breaks

  • The first-order speed is set by a local kernel: \(U_1 \propto \int_V\) 𝒦 \(\mathrm{d}V\), where 𝒦 \(= \boldsymbol{A}:\nabla\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\) (blue enhances, red reduces)

Sphere — 𝒦 antisymmetric, cancels exactly ⟹ \(U_1=0\)

Spheroid — curvature localizes 𝒦, antisymmetry broken ⟹ \(U_1\neq0\)

Finally, we would like to visualize what happens around the particle. Since the first-order speed correction is a volume integral, we just plot the integral kernel, K. Blue regions push the speed up, and red regions push it down. For the sphere, on the left, the Newtonian flow is symmetric about the equator, so all the blue and red regions cancel out exactly. However, for a spheroidal particle, we no longer have this symmetry. Particularly near the highly curved tip, the slip changes quickly, and the flow gradients become large. This localizes the kernel and breaks its antisymmetry. So the cancellation is no longer exact, and a net enhancement survives. And these are all the main results.

Summary

Geometry can qualitatively change non-Newtonian propulsion.

  • Shear thinning: Elongation can turn propulsion reduction into enhancement and break the complementary-coating symmetry.
  • Viscoelasticity: Elongation can produce strong propulsion enhancement and break the coverage antisymmetry.

To summarize. For shear thinning, elongation can reverse the rheological effect, changing propulsion reduction into enhancement. Geometric anisotropy also breaks the equality between complementary coatings.

For a viscoelastic fluid, we clarify that the vanishing correction for a sphere is a special case. It is a result of the geometric isotropy of the sphere. Once the particle becomes a spheroid, eccentricity breaks this symmetry. Especially for slender particles, the propulsion is enhanced in most cases.

More generally, we believe in a principle: symmetry breaking requires the combination of geometric anisotropy and fluid nonlinearity. In short, particle shape is a control parameter for propulsion in complex fluids. Thank you, and I am happy to take questions.
